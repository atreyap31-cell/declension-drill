Step one

The nominative lies. The genitive never does.

You cannot tell a noun's declension from the form you meet in a sentence, or even from its dictionary headword. You tell it from the genitive singular — which is exactly why every dictionary entry hands you two forms instead of one.

The genitive test
Gen. sg.Decl.Dictionary entryStem
-ae1stpuella, puellae, f.puell-
-ī2ndservus, servī, m. · bellum, bellī, n.serv- / bell-
-is3rdrēx, rēgis, m.rēg-

Three endings, three declensions, no overlap. Whatever the headword looks like, the genitive settles it.

Four rules that cut the memorising in half

  1. 1
    Build every form on the genitive stem, not the nominative.
    rēx, rēgis → drop -is → stem rēg-. Every remaining form is rēg- plus an ending: rēgem, rēgī, rēgēs, rēgibus. The nominative rēx is a one-off collision (rēg + s → x) — that is the whole reason the 3rd declension feels chaotic, and the only thing your chart's “modified stem” note means.
  2. 2
    The Neuter Law — no exceptions anywhere in Latin.
    Nom. = Acc. = Voc., in both numbers. And all three plurals always end in -a. So a neuter noun has only five distinct forms to learn, not twelve: bellum, bellum, bellum · bella, bella, bella.
  3. 3
    Dative plural = Ablative plural. Always.
    1st and 2nd: -īs. 3rd: -ibus. Four free cells in every paradigm.
  4. 4
    The ablative singular is the long-vowel case.
    1st -ā, 2nd -ō, 3rd -e (or -ī in i-stems). In the 1st declension the macron on -ā is the only thing separating ablative singular from nominative singular. Write your macrons.

Gender defaults — and the traps

1st Feminine — except words for traditionally male roles, which are masculine but decline exactly the same: agricola (farmer), nauta (sailor), poēta (poet), pīrāta (pirate), incola (inhabitant). Their adjectives go masculine; their endings do not change.

2nd -us and -er → masculine; -um → neuter. Reliable. Watch the -er nouns: some keep the e (puer, puerī) and some drop it (ager, agrī). Only the genitive tells you which.

3rd Anything. Gender has to be memorised with the word — that is why the entry bothers to print m., f., or n.

Spotting a 3rd-declension i-stem

The parenthesised endings on your chart are the i-stem forms. The reliable giveaway is -ium in the genitive plural.

M./F. i-stems — either the nominative and genitive singular have the same number of syllables (cīvis, cīvis), or the stem ends in two consonants (urbs, urbis → urbium). They take gen. pl. -ium, and may take acc. pl. -īs.

Neuter i-stems — nominative singular ends in -e, -al, or -ar (mare, animal, exemplar). These take the whole set: abl. sg. -ī, nom./acc./voc. pl. -ia, gen. pl. -ium.

Reference

Case endings, 1st – 3rd

Read the shortcuts off the chart

1st Twelve slots, six distinct forms. -ae alone fills four of them (gen. sg., dat. sg., nom. pl., voc. pl.); -a covers nom. and voc. sg.; -īs covers dat. and abl. pl. Learn six things, get twelve.

2nd M. The one place in the whole chart where the vocative differs from the nominative: -us becomes -e. Et tū, Brūte — never Brūtus.

Neuter Both neuter columns obey the Neuter Law, so rows 1, 4 and 6 of each block are identical. Learn -um / -ī / -ō and -a / -ōrum / -īs and the entire 2nd-neuter paradigm is done.

3rd -ibus handles dat. and abl. plural for both genders, and -ēs handles nom., acc. and voc. plural for M./F. Six of the twelve M./F. cells are just two endings.

Special topic

Neuters

Neuters are less to memorise than anything else on the chart — five distinct forms instead of twelve. What makes them hard is two separate things: the endings stop telling you the case, and the 3rd-declension nominatives can't be guessed from the stem. Each drill below attacks one of them.

The two families that cover most 3rd-declension neuters

Nom. sg.StemExamples
-us-or- / -er-corpus → corpor- · tempus → tempor- · opus → oper- · genus → gener- · vulnus → vulner-
-men-min-nōmen → nōmin- · flūmen → flūmin- · carmen → carmin- · lūmen → lūmin-
-e, -al, -ari-stemmare → mar- · animal → animāl- · exemplar → exemplār-

The -men family is perfectly regular: learn one, you have them all. The -us family has to be learned word by word — but knowing it is a family is most of the battle, because it stops you filing corpus with servus.

When the ending won't tell you the case

The Neuter Law makes Nom. = Acc. = Voc., so for a neuter you resolve the case from the sentence instead. Work down this list and stop at the first one that answers:

  1. 1
    Is the subject slot already taken?
    Another nominative in the sentence means your neuter is the object.
  2. 2
    What number is the verb?
    A plural verb with no other candidate makes a neuter plural the subject. A linking verb (est, sunt) plus a neuter adjective is the giveaway.
  3. 3
    Word order, as a tiebreaker.
    Latin's default is subject first, object immediately before the verb. Not a law, but right more often than not.

And the collision to watch: neuter plural -a looks exactly like 1st-declension singular -a. bella (wars) versus puella (girl). Both short — macrons will not save you. Only knowing the noun's gender will.

Parsing

Ending decoder

Translating is the reverse problem: you have an ending in front of you and need every case it could possibly be. Pick one and try to name all its jobs before you reveal.

-ae

The three that cause the most wrong translations

-a vs -ā — short -a is 1st nom./voc. sg., or any neuter nom./acc./voc. plural. Long -ā is 1st abl. sg. and nothing else. If your text prints macrons this is free; if it doesn't, let the verb and any preposition decide.

-is vs -īs — short -is is 3rd gen. singular ("of the …"). Long -īs is 1st/2nd dat. or abl. plural. Opposite number, opposite job, one macron apart.

-um — 2nd acc. sg. masculine, or 2nd neuter nom./acc./voc. sg., or 3rd gen. plural. If the sentence already has a direct object, suspect the genitive plural.
